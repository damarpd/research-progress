## Preliminary research gap synthesis

Cross-referencing the tables above against the study's working focus (adaptive passenger behavior — including *stationary* passengers, i.e. those remaining on-board and those waiting on the platform — during boarding/alighting under crowded and disruptive-event conditions):

1. **Empirical gap — stationary/remaining passengers under crowding.** Section A shows that only Yining Jia et al. (2026) explicitly categorizes passengers into boarding / alighting / remaining on-board, but their remaining-passenger behavior is calibrated on a *seated, normal-operations* experimental setting (Fu et al., 2023). None of the SFM-family studies (A) model how remaining on-board passengers or platform-waiting passengers *adapt* (reposition, yield, absorb crowding pressure) once density rises beyond normal operating conditions — the closest is Shen et al. (2026a), which treats platform waiters and carriage standees as *stationary force-generating obstacles*, not as adaptive agents with their own behavioral thresholds.
2. **Theoretical gap — SFM is physical, not attitudinal/decision-based.** Section B (Asano, Iryo & Kuwahara, 2009) explicitly argues that force-based models reproduce trajectories without explaining the underlying decision process. None of the reviewed SFM extensions (A) incorporate a decision/utility layer for *why* a stationary passenger chooses to yield vs. resist under crowding — this is where the DDCM strand (Fujiwara et al., n.d.; Urata & Hato, 2024) could complement SFM, but no paper in this set combines the two for the boarding/alighting context specifically.
3. **Contextual gap — disruptive/large-scale-event-induced crowding.** Section E papers study mega-event mode/route/timing choice at the trip level (Parkes et al., 2016; Mohammed et al., 2025) — none examine *microscopic* boarding/alighting passenger dynamics *during* such event-induced crowding spikes; conversely, the microscopic boarding/alighting studies (A, C) are all set in routine/normal operating conditions (non-peak or standard peak), not disruption- or event-induced extreme crowding.
4. **Measurement gap — linking subjective crowding to movement behavior.** Section F establishes that crowding is subjective and only weakly tied to objective density (Stokols, 1972; Kalb & Keating, 1981; Mohd Mahudin et al., 2012), but these studies measure *stress/satisfaction outcomes* via survey, not *movement/behavioral* outcomes. None of the movement-dynamics studies (A, C) incorporate a perceived-crowding construct as a driver of the adaptive behaviors they simulate.
5. **Setting gap.** No paper reviewed so far uses a Jakarta / Indonesian BRT or commuter-rail case (relevant if the study proceeds with a Jakarta case study, per `individual_note.md` 31/05 notes) — all metro/platform case studies are China (Beijing, Chengdu, Nanchang) or Hong Kong; bus dwell-time work is Singapore; sidewalk SFM work is Mumbai/Iran.

**Working implication:** the identified niche is a model of *adaptive behavior of stationary/remaining passenger types* (on-board and platform-waiting) under crowding intensified by disruptive/large-scale events, bridging the physical SFM layer (A) with a decision/attitudinal layer informed by DDCM (B) and perceived-crowding constructs (F) — see `individual_note.md` "Research gap and contribution" table (09/05–10/05 entry) for the current framing of this argument.
